# De-jargoning — the defining craft of this skill

This is the hardest and most important quality bar. A finance-fluent generalist — a chief
investment officer (CIO) who is not a biologist, physicist, chemist, or engineer — must be
able to read the memo and *fundamentally understand the business*. Every term from the
underlying science or engineering has to be translated. Finance and strategy vocabulary
(recurring revenue, switching costs, value chain, operating leverage) is fair game and needs
no translation. Domain-technical vocabulary is not.

## The five principles

1. **Faithful first, vivid second.** The analogy must preserve the *mechanism that matters
   for the business* — what they sell, to whom, and why it's hard or valuable. Vividness
   that distorts the mechanism is a failure. A vivid-but-wrong analogy is worse than honest
   jargon, because a CIO acting on a confidently-wrong picture is worse off than one who
   knows they don't understand.

2. **Keep the precise term in a parenthetical, right after the plain version.** This does
   three jobs at once: it satisfies the house rule to define every term at first use, it
   lets the reader verify the translation, and it lets a later domain-expert call catch a
   translation that drifted. Form: *plain-English mechanism (industry term: X)*.

3. **Translate the mechanism, not just the word.** Don't swap one piece of jargon for a
   slightly simpler piece of jargon. Get to the physical or biological *thing that happens*,
   then to why a customer pays for it.

4. **Don't smuggle in a value judgment.** "A revolutionary breakthrough drug that…" is the
   company's framing, not a translation. Translate what it *does*; judge the business
   separately, in the verdict.

5. **Flag the seam when you make a judgment call.** If translating required a real
   interpretive choice — you simplified away a distinction an expert would care about, or
   you're not certain the analogy holds — say so, and note it in the memo's open-questions
   section. Don't hide the seam.

## The test

Would a smart generalist who read *only* your sentence be able to (a) state what the company
sells and (b) not be embarrassed saying it in front of a domain expert? If either fails,
rewrite.

## Worked translations

These show the move from raw jargon → faithful plain English. Study the pattern; don't reuse
the wording literally.

**Biotech — bispecific T-cell engager**
- Raw: "a bispecific antibody targeting CD3 and BCMA via a T-cell engager mechanism."
- Decoded: "a drug that physically tethers the patient's own immune cells to cancer cells, so
  the immune system destroys them — like handcuffing the attacker to the target (industry
  term: bispecific T-cell engager). They sell it to hospitals treating blood cancers."

**Biotech — antibody-drug conjugate (ADC)**
- Decoded: "a guided missile for cancer: a tumour-seeking antibody carrying a tiny toxic
  payload that only detonates once it's inside the cancer cell, sparing healthy tissue
  (industry term: antibody-drug conjugate, or ADC)."

**Biotech — gene editing**
- Decoded: "molecular find-and-replace for DNA — a tool that locates a specific faulty gene
  and cuts or rewrites it (industry term: CRISPR gene editing). The pitch is a one-time fix
  for diseases caused by a single broken gene."

**Photonics — silicon photonics / optical transceiver**
- Raw: "co-packaged optics enabling 1.6T pluggable transceivers for hyperscale fabrics."
- Decoded: "they move data with light instead of electricity. Inside a data centre, the part
  that converts a chip's electrical 1s and 0s into pulses of light to fire down a glass
  fibre — and back again — is the on-ramp/off-ramp between the computer and the cable
  (industry term: optical transceiver). As artificial-intelligence (AI) data centres move
  more data, faster and hotter, doing this with light rather than copper wiring becomes the
  only way to keep up. They sell these parts to the companies building those data centres."

**Semiconductors — extreme-ultraviolet (EUV) lithography**
- Decoded: "the machine that prints the circuitry onto the most advanced computer chips,
  using light so fine it can draw lines a few atoms wide (industry term: EUV lithography).
  One Dutch company is the only firm on earth that makes it — which makes them a choke point
  for the entire leading-edge chip industry."

**Industrials — precision reducer / harmonic drive**
- Raw: "high-precision strain-wave gearing for articulated robot joints."
- Decoded: "the special gearbox inside a robot's joint that lets a fast-spinning motor move
  the arm slowly, smoothly, and with extreme precision — without it the arm would be jerky
  and imprecise (industry term: precision reducer / harmonic drive). Only a handful of firms
  make the good ones, so a large share of the world's robot arms have their part inside."

**Chemicals — electrolyzer**
- Decoded: "a machine that uses electricity to split water into hydrogen and oxygen, so you
  can make hydrogen fuel from power and water rather than from natural gas (industry term:
  electrolyzer). They sell the machines to companies trying to produce 'clean' hydrogen."

**Fintech infrastructure — interchange / payment rails**
- Decoded: "every time a shopper taps a card, a small fee is split among the banks and
  networks that move the money; this company sits in that pipe and takes a sliver of each
  transaction (industry term: interchange on the payment rails). Their revenue rises simply
  with the number and size of transactions flowing through."

## When the science genuinely matters to the thesis

For a pre-revenue biotech, the science *is* the business — there's no product yet, only a
mechanism and a clinical path. Translate the mechanism carefully, then translate the
*path*: what has to be proven, in what order, and what a success or failure at each gate
would mean in plain terms (e.g., "a mid-stage human trial reading out next year is the event
the whole company hinges on — industry term: Phase 2 readout"). Resist the temptation to go
deeper into the biology than the business decision requires.

## What NOT to do

- Don't leave a single untranslated technical term sitting in the memo "because everyone
  knows it." Assume the reader does not.
- Don't translate into a *different* jargon ("it uses a transformer architecture" → "it uses
  a neural net" is not a translation).
- Don't let the analogy run away from the facts for the sake of a good line.
- Don't translate finance terms — that condescends to the reader. Keep the register adult.
