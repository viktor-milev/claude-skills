# Industry Lenses

The twelve-section memo spine is universal. What changes by industry is *emphasis* — which
sections carry the weight, what jargon needs the most translation, where fragility usually
hides, and what shape revenue usually takes. Read the matching section at framing. If none
fits cleanly, apply the spine directly with first-principles judgment.

## Contents
1. Biotech / pharma
2. Hardware / deep-tech (photonics, optics, semiconductors, semi-cap, robotics, industrials)
3. Software / platforms

5. Consumer / services

## 1. Biotech / pharma

The science *is* the business, especially pre-revenue. Spend the translation budget here.

- **What they do / How they make money:** distinguish pre-revenue (a mechanism and a clinical
  path, no product) from commercial (an approved drug, real sales). For pre-revenue, revenue
  "shape" is really a *milestone path*: regulatory and trial gates, not subscriptions.
- **The need / vanish test:** what disease, what unmet need, what patients do today instead.
- **Tailwind:** scientific platform durability vs single-asset bet; demographics; regulatory
  tailwinds. Watch for "platform" claims that are really one molecule.
- **Fragility (heavy):** binary clinical/regulatory readouts; **cash runway as an existence
  question** (how long until they must raise money or die — state it as a structural fact,
  not a valuation); patent cliffs; single-asset / single-trial concentration; reimbursement
  risk (will payers actually pay).
- **Ownership:** founder-scientist key-person risk; big-pharma partnerships and who controls
  the asset.
- **Jargon to translate:** mechanism of action, modality (small molecule / antibody / cell /
  gene therapy), trial phases and endpoints, the regulator's gates.

## 2. Hardware / deep-tech

Photonics, optics, semiconductors, semiconductor capital equipment, robotics, specialist
industrials. The heaviest physics/engineering translation load.

- **What they do:** translate the physical mechanism and *where in a system their part sits*
  (the on-ramp, the gearbox, the choke-point machine). Name what the customer's product
  can't do without it.
- **How they make money:** often a mix — upfront equipment/component sale plus a sticky
  **aftermarket** (service, spares, consumables: the razor/blade); **design wins** that lock
  in years of future volume; capex-driven, **cyclical** demand.
- **Value-chain power (critical):** are they the toll booth (a bottleneck everyone must pass
  through — ASML, a sole-source component) or a toll payer (a commodity assembler)? This is
  usually the whole "good business" question for hardware.
- **Fragility:** customer concentration; exposure to a single end-market's capex cycle;
  technology-transition risk (a node shift, a new standard) that can strand a product.
- **Tailwind exposure-quality test (important):** many claim AI/electrification exposure;
  check whether it actually flows through to *their* revenue or is adjacency.
- **Jargon to translate:** the device physics, fab/process terms, throughput and precision
  specs, standards and form factors.

## 3. Software / platforms

Software-as-a-service (SaaS), marketplaces, infrastructure, app platforms.

- **How they make money:** recurring-revenue *character* — subscription vs usage-based vs
  take-rate on a marketplace. Describe the shape and stickiness qualitatively; net retention
  is *character* ("customers tend to spend more over time"), not a number to quote.
- **Why they win:** switching costs (data, integrations, workflow lock-in), network effects
  (does each new user make it better for the others), and the platform-vs-feature question
  (is this a durable platform or one feature away from being absorbed by a bigger suite).
- **The do-nothing competitor:** for software the real alternative is often a spreadsheet or
  an internal script — name it.
- **Fragility:** platform-dependence (built on top of someone else's store/cloud/API who can
  change the rules), customer concentration, commoditization by a hyperscaler.
- **Jargon to translate:** less hard science, more architecture buzzwords — translate them to
  "what job it does for the customer," and don't let "AI-native / agentic / cloud-native"
  pass untranslated.

## 4. Financials / cyclicals

Banks, insurers, exchanges, asset managers, commodities, miners, energy.

- **How they make money:** name the engine plainly — a **spread** business (borrow cheap,
  lend dearer), a **float** business (hold others' money and invest it — insurers), a **toll**
  business (a fee per transaction — exchanges), or a **price-taker** (commodities/miners sell
  at a price the market sets, so the only levers are volume and cost).
- **Cyclicality (heavy):** for commodities/miners/energy the demand and price character
  dominates everything — counter-cyclical, deeply cyclical, or secular.
- **Regulatory dependence:** for banks/insurers/exchanges, the regulator is a structural
  feature of the business, not a footnote — capital rules, licences, who can compete.
- **Fragility:** leverage as a structural fact (state it qualitatively — "this business runs
  on borrowed money by design, so confidence is load-bearing"); concentration; commodity
  price exposure; a single regulator's rule.
- **Jargon to translate:** mostly finance (fair game) — but translate the *commodity/energy*
  physical terms and any insurance/reinsurance mechanics.

## 5. Consumer / services

Brands, retail, restaurants, consumer health, consumer services.

- **The need:** staple (bought regardless) vs discretionary (first cut in a downturn) is the
  central axis — it shapes the whole demand character.
- **Why they win:** brand as a moat (does the name let them charge more or sell more
  reliably), distribution, scale, habit.
- **How they make money:** the unit of demand (a repeat purchase, a visit, a subscription
  box) and how repeatable it is.
- **Fragility:** fashion/fad risk, input-cost exposure, channel concentration (one big
  retailer), private-label competition.
- **Jargon:** usually light — keep the register adult and don't over-explain.
